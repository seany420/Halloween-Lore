00HOW TO USE THIS

This is organized for someone writing into the franchise, not reading about it. Four ways in:

  • Lost? Start with The Continuity Problem. Halloween isn't one story — it's five mutually exclusive stories that share a first act.
  • Need facts fast? Film Dossiers gives you plot, cast, kills, and continuity flags per film.
  • Need to invent? The Rules of the Shape, Lore & Mythology, and Haddonfield are the load-bearing walls. Break them knowingly.
  • Need an opening? Open Seams lists the years, places, and people the films never accounted for. That's where your story fits without fighting anyone.

A note on accuracy: body counts and minor character fates vary between theatrical cuts, unrated cuts, TV cuts, and the Producer's Cut. Where a number is contested it's marked ≈. Where a fact only exists in one cut, that's flagged.

01THE CONTINUITY PROBLEM

Thirteen films. No single continuity contains more than six of them. Every entry after 1981 is a fork, and most forks pretend the others don't exist.

The one fixed point: Halloween (1978). Everything descends from it.

The five timelines

A · THORN (the original six) 1978 → 1981 → 4 (1988) → 5 (1989) → 6 (1995) The longest continuous run. Laurie is Michael's sister; she dies offscreen in a 1987 car crash; her daughter Jamie Lloyd inherits the target. Ends with an ancient druidic curse as the explicit engine. Loomis is present throughout.

B · H20 (the Curtis line) 1978 → 1981 → H20 (1998) → Resurrection (2002) Skips 4, 5, and 6 entirely — Jamie Lloyd, Thorn, and the Man in Black never happened. Laurie faked her death and is alive under an assumed name. Loomis is dead. Ends with Michael in a morgue drawer.

C · ZOMBIE (the remakes) Halloween (2007) → Halloween II (2009) A closed system. Nothing carries over from any prior film. Michael has a documented abusive childhood and an explicit psychological etiology; Loomis is a self-promoting author; the supernatural returns as hallucinated maternal visitation.

D · GREEN / BLUMHOUSE (the legacy trilogy) 1978 → 2018 → Kills (2021) → Ends (2022) The most aggressive reset: everything after 1978 is deleted, including the sibling relationship, which a character explicitly dismisses as a rumor. Loomis died at some point offscreen. Michael is killed onscreen, publicly, in front of the town.

E · SEASON OF THE WITCH (the orphan) Halloween III (1982) — standalone. No Michael. In this film's world, Halloween (1978) is a movie playing on television, which makes Michael Myers fictional inside it. The original anthology plan: a new unrelated horror story every October. It didn't take.

What every timeline agrees on

  1. Halloween night, 1963: six-year-old Michael Myers kills his teenage sister Judith in the family home.
  2. Fifteen years of institutionalization under Dr. Sam Loomis.
  3. October 1978: he escapes, returns to Haddonfield, and attaches himself to Laurie Strode.
  4. He cannot be reasoned with, and shooting him is insufficient.

Everything else is negotiable. This is your permission slip.

03FILM DOSSIERS

HALLOWEEN (1978)A B D

Continuity: Origin — feeds A, B, D Director: John Carpenter · Writers: Carpenter & Debra Hill · Released: October 25, 1978 Budget: ≈$300,000 · Gross: ≈$70M worldwide

Plot. Halloween night 1963: a POV shot moves through the Myers house, up the stairs, through a clown mask, and stabs seventeen-year-old Judith Myers. The camera pulls back to reveal a six-year-old boy holding the knife. Fifteen years later Michael escapes during a transfer, steals a mask and rope and knives from a hardware store, and returns to Haddonfield. He shadows high-schooler Laurie Strode through a single day, kills her two friends and one boyfriend, and attacks her in the house where she's babysitting Tommy Doyle. She fights him off with a knitting needle, a coat hanger, and his own knife. Loomis shoots him six times off a second-floor balcony. When they look down, he's gone.

Kills (5). Judith Myers (1963) · a truck driver (offscreen, for coveralls) · Annie Brackett · Bob Simms · Lynda van der Klok.

Principal cast. Laurie Strode (Jamie Lee Curtis) · Dr. Sam Loomis (Donald Pleasence) · Annie Brackett (Nancy Kyes) · Lynda (P.J. Soles) · Sheriff Leigh Brackett (Charles Cyphers) · Tommy Doyle · Lindsey Wallace · Nurse Marion Chambers.

Production facts a writer can use.

  • The mask is a Captain Kirk mask by Don Post Studios, bought for around $2 and modified by production designer Tommy Lee Wallace — eye holes widened, hair teased and painted, the whole thing sprayed a dead flat white.
  • Credited as "The Shape," not Michael Myers. Nick Castle wore it for most of the film; Tony Moran played the unmasked face in the reveal.
  • Working title: The Babysitter Murders. Producer Irwin Yablans supplied the holiday hook.
  • Shot in South Pasadena, California, in spring — hence palm trees in the background and painted leaves scattered, collected, and re-scattered between takes.
  • Carpenter's score is in 5/4, which is why it never lets you settle.
  • There is no motive. Loomis offers a theological diagnosis rather than a psychological one. Everything the franchise later does is an attempt to fill this vacuum, and every attempt costs something.

HALLOWEEN II (1981)A B

Continuity: A and B Director: Rick Rosenthal · Writers: Carpenter & Hill · Released: October 30, 1981

Plot. Resumes at the exact second the first film ends. Laurie is taken to Haddonfield Memorial Hospital; Michael follows and works through the night staff while Loomis searches the town. Midway, Loomis learns Laurie is Michael's younger sister — sealed adoption records, dead parents, a name change. The film ends with Loomis igniting an operating room full of ether and oxygen, burning himself and Michael alive.

Kills (≈10). Alice Martin · Mrs. Alves (blood drained) · Budd Scarmon · Nurse Karen (scalded in the hydrotherapy tank) · Janet (syringe to the temple) · Dr. Mixter · Mr. Garrett (claw hammer) · Nurse Jill Franco · plus Ben Tramer, killed indirectly when a squad car strikes him and he burns — mistaken for Michael, and the franchise's first suggestion that panic kills as efficiently as Michael does.

What it introduces — and what it costs.

  • The sibling retcon. Now there's a reason, and the reason is blood. Carpenter later expressed regret; two subsequent timelines are built specifically to escape it.
  • SAMHAIN, scrawled in blood on a school chalkboard. First supernatural crack in the film's realism. It goes unexplained for fourteen years.
  • The hospital-as-labyrinth template that half the franchise reruns.

HALLOWEEN III: SEASON OF THE WITCH (1982)E

Continuity: E — standalone Director: Tommy Lee Wallace · Story: Nigel Kneale (uncredited after rewrites)

Plot. Dr. Daniel Challis and Ellie Grimbridge investigate her father's murder and trace it to Silver Shamrock Novelties in Santa Mira, California — a company town run by Conal Cochran, who has embedded a fragment of a stolen Stonehenge trilith into microchips in three children's masks (pumpkin, skull, witch). A televised "Big Giveaway" broadcast will trigger them nationwide, killing every child wearing one — a mass sacrifice restoring Samhain to its original function. The film ends with Challis screaming into a payphone to pull the third channel, and no confirmation that anyone does.

Why it matters to you. It's the only entry that takes the holiday itself as the antagonist rather than the man. It also contains the franchise's most quietly radical idea: Halloween is a domesticated ritual that still remembers what it was for. If you want lore that isn't Michael, this is the vein.

HALLOWEEN 4: THE RETURN OF MICHAEL MYERS (1988)A

Continuity: A Director: Dwight H. Little

Plot. Ten years comatose at Ridgemont Federal Sanitarium. During a transfer, Michael overhears that he has a niece and wakes. Jamie Lloyd, orphaned by Laurie's 1987 death, lives with the Carruthers family and her foster sister Rachel. Michael kills the power station and blacks out Haddonfield; a vigilante mob forms and murders an innocent man. State police shoot Michael off a pickup truck and he falls down a mine shaft. Final scene: Jamie, in a clown costume, stabs her foster mother with a pair of scissors. Loomis raises his gun and screams.

Kills (≈17). Ambulance crew · gas-station attendant and mechanic · Kelly Meeker (shotgun, pinned to a wall) · Deputy Logan · Brady · power station crew · Bucky the electrician. Ted Hollister is killed by the mob, not Michael.

Key introductions. Jamie Lloyd · Rachel Carruthers · Sheriff Ben Meeker · the mob as a second monster (revived wholesale in Kills) · the closing implication that the thing is heritable.

HALLOWEEN 5: THE REVENGE OF MICHAEL MYERS (1989)A

Continuity: A Director: Dominique Othenin-Girard

Plot. Michael floated out of the mine and was nursed for a year by a hermit, whom he kills on waking. Jamie is now mute, institutionalized, and psychically linked to him — she seizes when he kills. Loomis, past the point of ethics, uses her as bait in the Myers house. Michael is subdued and jailed. Then a figure in black boots — glimpsed throughout, marked with a Thorn tattoo — shoots the entire station and walks him out.

Kills (≈15). The hermit · Rachel Carruthers (scissors) · Tina Williams · Samantha and Spitz (pitchforked together in a barn) · Deputies Nick Ross and Tom Farrah · clinic staff.

The moment worth stealing. Jamie calls him uncle, and asks him to take off the mask. He does. He cries. She touches his face. He tries to kill her about four seconds later. It is the only time the franchise lets the thing be a person, and it does not survive contact with itself.

HALLOWEEN: THE CURSE OF MICHAEL MYERS (1995)A

Continuity: A — closes it Director: Joe Chappelle · Note: exists in two substantially different versions

Plot (theatrical). Six years on, Jamie has been held by a cult and forcibly impregnated. She escapes with the infant, hides him in a bus station restroom, and is killed. Tommy Doyle — the boy from 1978, now grown and obsessive — lives across from the Myers house, which is occupied by the Strode family. The Thorn curse is laid out: a druidic rune, one child of each tribe chosen to bear it, required to sacrifice their own bloodline on Samhain so the tribe is spared. Dr. Terence Wynn — the Smith's Grove administrator glimpsed in 1978 — is the Man in Black. Michael slaughters the lab and Tommy beats him down with a lead pipe; only the mask remains.

Plot (Producer's Cut). Substantially different third act. Michael is stopped by runic stones and a ritual rather than a pipe. Wynn's role is expanded and he dies at Michael's hands. The film ends with the Thorn mark appearing on Loomis's wrist — the curse transfers to him. It is the more coherent film and the darker ending.

Notes. Donald Pleasence died in February 1995, before release; the film is dedicated to him. Paul Rudd plays Tommy Doyle. The Producer's Cut circulated on bootleg for two decades before an official release, and much of the fandom treats it as the real version.

Why it matters. This is the franchise's one full commitment to explaining Michael. It answers the question and, in answering it, demonstrates why nobody should. Read it as a cautionary document.

HALLOWEEN H20: 20 YEARS LATER (1998)B

Continuity: B Director: Steve Miner

Plot. Michael ransacks the home of Nurse Marion Whittington to steal Laurie's sealed file, kills her and two neighbors, and drives to California. Laurie is Keri Tate, headmistress of a private boarding school, functional alcoholic, mother of a seventeen-year-old son she smothers. When the school empties for a field trip, she stops running. The last sequence is Laurie deliberately going back in with a fire axe, and ends with her decapitating him at a guardrail.

Kills (≈6). Marion Whittington · Jimmy Howell · Tony Allegre · Charlie Deveraux · Sarah Wainthrope · Will Brennan.

Notes. Janet Leigh appears as a school secretary and drives the same 1957 Ford she drove in Psycho. Jamie Lee Curtis pushed for the 20th-anniversary return; Kevin Williamson consulted on the story, and the film's self-awareness shows it. Cameo by a very young Joseph Gordon-Levitt in the opening.

The thesis. Survivorhood as a chronic condition. Twenty years of hypervigilance framed as pathology by everyone around her, and then vindicated. Every legacy sequel since has copied this and none has improved on it.

HALLOWEEN: RESURRECTION (2002)B

Continuity: B — closes it Director: Rick Rosenthal

Plot. A prologue undoes the decapitation: the man Laurie killed was a paramedic whose larynx Michael crushed before swapping clothes. Laurie, institutionalized and faking catatonia, nearly wins — and hesitates for two seconds to confirm the mask. He kills her. The main film is Dangertainment, a live internet broadcast placing six college students in the Myers house with helmet cameras. Michael works through them. The final shot is his eyes opening in the morgue.

Kills (≈8). Laurie Strode · Nora Winston · Bill · Jen · Rudy · Donna · Jim · plus a cameraman.

Notes. Widely regarded as the franchise's low point. Its one durable idea is Halloween as content — the house monetized, the trauma streamed, an audience watching real deaths and assuming they're staged. That idea aged extremely well, and Kills/Ends both circle it without landing it. If you want a modern hook that isn't a legacy sequel, it's sitting right here, unclaimed.

HALLOWEEN (2007) — Rob ZombieC

Continuity: C Director/Writer: Rob Zombie

Plot. Roughly the first half is Michael's childhood: a squalid house, an abusive stepfather, a stripper mother who loves him, a school bully, and a boy who tortures animals and photographs them. He kills the bully, then on Halloween kills his stepfather, his sister's boyfriend, and his sister. At Smith's Grove he makes papier-mâché masks compulsively, kills a nurse, and goes permanently silent; his mother kills herself. Fifteen years later he escapes and walks home to find the baby sister he was separated from — Laurie Strode. Loomis is a failed clinician turned true-crime author.

What it changes. Motive becomes etiology: abuse, poverty, cruelty, an institution that fails him. Michael is enormous and physical rather than uncanny. Loomis is compromised rather than prophetic. Unrated and theatrical cuts differ significantly, including the ending and a Smith's Grove assault sequence.

HALLOWEEN II (2009) — Rob ZombieC

Continuity: C — closes it Director/Writer: Rob Zombie

Plot. Laurie, living with Annie and Sheriff Brackett, is disintegrating: nightmares, rage, therapy that isn't working. Michael has spent a year as a bearded drifter, guided by visions of his dead mother in white leading a white horse. Loomis is on a book tour publicly outing Laurie as Michael's sister. It ends at a shed on a farm with Loomis dead, Michael shot, and Laurie in an institution wearing the mask and smiling at a vision of her mother.

What it changes. The horror is inheritance and psychiatric collapse, not stalking. It is the only entry primarily interested in the survivor's interior. It is also the only one where the adult Michael speaks — a single word.

HALLOWEEN (2018)D

Continuity: D Director: David Gordon Green · Writers: Green, Danny McBride, Jeff Fradley

Plot. Two podcasters bring the mask to Smith's Grove and then to Laurie, who has spent forty years preparing: a house with floodlights, a gun room, a basement bunker, three failed marriages, a daughter the state took away at twelve. The transfer bus crashes. Michael walks back into Haddonfield. Dr. Sartain, Loomis's protégé, turns out to be an acolyte and dies for it. The finale traps Michael in Laurie's burning basement — three generations of Strode women running him into a cage they built on purpose.

Kills (≈18). The podcasters · a gas station mechanic · Vicky · Dave · Oscar · Officer Francis · Dr. Sartain · a father and son in a truck · assorted.

What it changes. The sibling relationship is explicitly discarded — dismissed onscreen as something people made up. Motive returns to zero. Loomis is dead. Frank Hawkins, the deputy who found Michael in 1978, is retconned in as a witness.

HALLOWEEN KILLS (2021)D

Continuity: D Director: David Gordon Green

Plot. Firefighters cut Michael out of the burning basement and he kills all of them. Laurie spends the film in a hospital bed. Tommy Doyle raises a mob under the chant evil dies tonight, and the mob corners an innocent escaped patient who jumps to his death rather than be torn apart. Karen retrieves the mask and is killed in the Myers house. A 1978 flashback expands Hawkins's night: his partner Pete McCabe, a mistake, and Loomis preventing him from letting Michael die.

Kills (≈27). Highest count in the franchise. Includes Tommy Doyle, Marion Chambers, Lonnie Elam, Vanessa and Marcus, Sondra, Big John and Little John, and Karen Nelson.

Thesis. Trauma as civic contagion. The town becomes the monster and Michael barely has to work. Divisive, but the most thematically legible entry after 1978.

HALLOWEEN ENDS (2022)D

Continuity: D — closes it Director: David Gordon Green

Plot. A 2019 prologue: Corey Cunningham babysits a boy who dies in a fall; acquitted, he becomes the town's designated monster. Four years later Corey and Allyson fall in love, and Corey — beaten by locals and thrown into a storm drain — meets a weakened, hiding Michael, who doesn't kill him. Something passes between them. Corey starts killing, takes the mask, and turns on Michael. Laurie finally kills Michael in her own kitchen with a knife and a broken arm, and the town watches his body go into an auto shredder.

What it changes. Evil becomes transmissible — an appetite the town keeps feeding, capable of finding a new host. It abandons the slasher structure almost entirely for two-thirds of its runtime and remains the franchise's most argued-about film.

FRANCHISE STATUS (as of 2026)

Rights sit with Miramax (film, jointly) and Trancas International Films (Malek Akkad; TV). Trancas solely owns the television rights while Miramax co-owns the film rights. Miramax won a 2023 bidding war for a TV series intended to launch an integrated film-and-television universe. That series has since reportedly been shelved in favor of a feature film as the starting point for a full creative reset, with a rumored 2028 release — unconfirmed. The Universal/Blumhouse partnership ended with Ends.

Practical upshot for you: nothing new is locked in. The field is as open as it has been since 2016.

04CHARACTER ENCYCLOPEDIA

The constants

MICHAEL MYERS / THE SHAPE — b. c. 1957. Silent, 6'1"–6'8" depending on the performer, walks rather than runs, wears mechanic's coveralls he takes from someone he kills. Credited as "The Shape" in 1978, 1981, and the Green trilogy. Not once, in any film, does he explain himself.

DR. SAMUEL LOOMIS — Michael's psychiatrist from age six. The franchise's engine of exposition and its moral casualty: fifteen years of failed treatment converted into a conviction that his patient is not a patient. Escalates across films from warning people to using a mute child as bait. Dies in 1981 (Timeline B), survives to 1995 and possibly inherits the curse (Timeline A), and in the Zombie films is a fraud selling the story. Donald Pleasence played him in five films; his death in 1995 ended the character's original run.

LAURIE STRODE — Babysitter, honor student, and the template for the American final girl. Four incompatible fates: dies offscreen in a 1987 car crash (A); fakes that same crash and lives to 2002 (B); is Angel Myers, institutionalized and wearing the mask (C); survives to 2022 and kills him herself (D).

JUDITH MYERS — 17, the first kill, the reason the house is a landmark. Her headstone recurs as a prop — Michael steals it in 1978 and arranges it over a body.

NURSE MARION CHAMBERS (later Whittington) — Present at the 1978 escape, and one of the very few characters to appear across timelines B and D. Dies in both.

Timeline A · Thorn

Jamie Lloyd — Laurie's daughter, orphaned at ~7. Stabs her foster mother in 1988, mute and psychically linked in 1989, a mother at ~15 in 1995, killed the same night. Rachel Carruthers — Jamie's foster sister; the closest the sequels get to a second Laurie. Killed in 1989. Sheriff Ben Meeker · Kelly Meeker · Tina Williams · Billy Hill The Man in Black / Dr. Terence Wynn — Smith's Grove administrator, background presence in 1978, revealed in 1995 as head of the Cult of Thorn. Tommy Doyle — The boy Laurie was babysitting. Returns in 1995 as an obsessive amateur researcher living opposite the house. The Strodes — Kara, her son Danny, brother Tim, parents John and Debra. Living in the Myers house. Steven Lloyd — Jamie's infant son. Survives 1995. Never accounted for again in any medium. He would be about thirty-one now.

Timeline B · H20

John Tate (Laurie's son) · Molly Cartwell · Will Brennan · Ronny Jones · Norma Watson Freddie Harris and Nora Winston — Dangertainment producers. Freddie survives. Sara Moyer — final girl of Resurrection.

Timeline C · Zombie

Deborah Myers — Michael's mother; the film's only sympathetic adult, and later his hallucinated guide. Ronnie White — abusive stepfather. Angel Myers — the infant sister, raised as Laurie Strode. Sheriff Lee Brackett and Annie Brackett — Annie survives the first film, badly injured; dies in the second.

Timeline D · Green

Karen Nelson — Laurie's daughter, removed from her care at twelve, spends 2018 as the family's skeptic and dies in 2021 seconds after admitting her mother was right. Allyson Nelson — granddaughter. The only Strode standing at the end. Frank Hawkins — the 1978 deputy, retconned into the original night. Dr. Ranbir Sartain — the "new Loomis," secretly an acolyte. Corey Cunningham — pariah, apprentice, and the franchise's only successful transfer of the mask. Lindsey Wallace — the other 1978 child, aged into Kills. Cameron and Lonnie Elam — Lonnie is the bully from 1978, grown.

05THE RULES OF THE SHAPE

Not stated in any film. Derived from what he consistently does. Break them deliberately; contradict them accidentally and readers will feel it.

Movement. He walks. He never runs, never sprints, and still arrives before you. He appears in frame behind people who just checked behind them. He is regularly out of focus in the background before anyone notices him — the single most imitated shot in the genre.

Sound. Breath through latex. No dialogue in eleven of thirteen films. When he does make noise it's exertion, never expression.

Sight. He watches for a full day before acting. The 1978 film is largely a film about a man standing still in daylight while nobody reacts correctly.

Competence. He drives a car after fifteen years institutionalized (Loomis: "someone taught him"). He uses tools, cuts phone lines, kills power stations, and stages bodies with theatrical care — the Wallace house tableau with the headstone is composed for an audience of one.

Durability. Six gunshots and a two-story fall. Fire and an explosion. A mine shaft. Decapitation (retconned). Repeated stabbing. He is stopped only twice in a way that sticks: by a ritual (Curse, Producer's Cut) and by industrial machinery in front of witnesses (Ends).

Selection. He goes home. Everything he does organizes around 45 Lampkin Lane and around one woman he has decided on, for reasons that vary by timeline. The people between him and her are incidental.

Restraint. He does not kill children. Tommy, Lindsey, Jamie, Danny, Julian — all spared or ignored. This holds in every continuity and it is the closest thing he has to a rule of his own.

Failure conditions. He loses when the survivor stops running and chooses the ground: the closet, the school, the fortified house, the kitchen. Fleeing prolongs it. Only staging works.

06LORE & MYTHOLOGY

Samhain

Introduced 1981 as a word in blood on a chalkboard, glossed onscreen as the Celtic festival of the dead and, loosely, its lord. The film's etymology is shaky — Samhain is the festival, not a deity — and that inaccuracy is itself franchise canon, repeated by characters who should know better. Functionally it means: the night the boundary thins, and something crosses to collect.

The Curse of Thorn (Timeline A)

Laid out in 1995. Thorn is a druidic rune associated with a constellation that appears only on certain Halloweens. When it rises, one child of each tribe is marked and compelled to sacrifice their own family — the entire bloodline — on Samhain night. In exchange, the tribe is spared famine, plague, and the death of the rest. It is not evil in the film's framing; it is a fixed price, paid in the same currency every generation.

The Cult of Thorn maintains the arrangement across centuries, embedded inside Smith's Grove, using the sanitarium's clinical authority as cover and its research budget as infrastructure. Dr. Wynn leads it. Michael's kills are compulsory. His inability to stop at Laurie, at Jamie, at Steven, is the curse working, not his appetite.

Why it's structurally useful: it converts Michael from an agent into an instrument, and it makes the institution complicit. Why it's dangerous: it makes him sympathetic, which drains him.

The Novelization Curse (1979)

Curtis Richards's tie-in novel predates Thorn by sixteen years and offers a rival origin: a boy in ancient Ireland, cursed for a transgression at the Samhain rites, condemned to be reborn across generations to kill. Not film canon, and the older, cleaner version of the idea. If you want lineage without the cult apparatus, this is the source to mine.

The Mask

Latex, blank, sexless, an emptied white face. Never the same twice — it ages, chars, splits, and is rebuilt film to film, and the fandom's obsessive cataloguing of these variations is itself part of the mythology. Symbolically it functions as negative space: it doesn't conceal a face, it removes one. In Ends it works as a relic — it can be worn by someone else and it works.

The White Horse (Timeline C)

Zombie's addition, quoted from a symbolism text: the white horse represents rage. Delivered by Michael's dead mother in visions. It's the only mythology in the franchise sourced from psychiatric iconography rather than folklore, and it's a clean model for a story that wants inheritance without druids.

Silver Shamrock (Timeline E)

Corporate Samhain. A stolen fragment of Stonehenge, mass production, and a national television broadcast used to perform an ancient child sacrifice at scale. The idea that the holiday's marketing apparatus is the ritual apparatus is the most portable concept in the entire franchise and almost nobody has used it since 1982.

07HADDONFIELD

Fictional town in Illinois, named for Haddonfield, New Jersey, where Debra Hill grew up. Population implied small: one hospital, one sheriff, everyone knows the house.

45 Lampkin Lane — the Myers house. Derelict after 1963, occupied at various points by the Strodes (1995) and by a gay couple who bought and restored it (2021). Local kids dare each other onto the porch. It is the franchise's true fixed point; more scenes happen there than anywhere else.

Other geography. Chestnut and Market Streets · Haddonfield Memorial Hospital · Haddonfield High School · Nichols Hardware · the Myers family cemetery (Judith's plot) · storm drains beneath the town (2022) · the mine shaft outside town (1988) · a working power station and, apparently, one grid switch for the whole town.

Smith's Grove–Warren County Sanitarium — 150 miles from Haddonfield. Michael's home for fifteen (or forty) years. Every timeline treats it as negligent at minimum: escapes during transfers, a cult in its administration, doctors who cross over into worship. In no version of this story is the institution innocent.

Nearby: Russellville · Langdon · Ridgemont Federal Sanitarium (1988).

Weather and texture. Overcast, leaf-choked, gray. Halloween decorations up on every porch, which means Michael is only ever slightly out of place. The whole design of the original is that he is camouflaged by the holiday.

Open seams — where a new story fits without a fight

  • 1978–1988, Timeline A. Ten years of a comatose man in a federal facility, with a cult inside the system that owns him. Almost entirely unwritten.
  • Steven Lloyd. Michael's grand-nephew, born 1995, survives, never mentioned again. Would be in his early thirties. The cleanest untouched bloodline in the franchise.
  • 1989–1995, Timeline A. Jamie's six years in cult custody. Referenced, never shown.
  • The Producer's Cut ending. Loomis carrying the Thorn mark. A whole film exists on the other side of that shot and nobody has made it.
  • Timeline E. Season of the Witch was designed as an anthology door and it's still standing open. Any Halloween-night story with no Michael in it is technically in continuity.
  • Anyone outside Haddonfield. Michael has left Illinois exactly once (1998). Nothing prevents the story from happening elsewhere, to someone unconnected, which the franchise has never seriously tried.
  • Halloween as content. Resurrection raised streamed atrocity in 2002 and fumbled it. Twenty-four years of infrastructure have been built since.

09BEYOND THE FILMS

Alternate cuts. Halloween II has an extended TV version with additional scenes. Curse of Michael Myers has the theatrical cut and the Producer's Cut (rune ritual, expanded Wynn, Loomis marked). Both Zombie films have unrated cuts with different endings. Halloween (1978) has a TV cut with Carpenter-shot inserts, including the Smith's Grove scene establishing the sibling link retroactively.

Novels. Curtis Richards, Halloween (1979) — the Irish curse origin. Dennis Etchison (as Jack Martin) novelized II and III. Nicholas Grabowsky handled 4. John Passarella novelized the 2018 film. Various 1990s Berkley original novels expand Thorn and are non-canonical.

Comics. Chaos! Comics ran a Halloween line in 2000–2001. Devil's Due published Halloween: The First Death of Laurie Strode and Nightdance in the late 2000s — Nightdance in particular is a genuine attempt at Michael as an uncanny presence rather than a slasher.

Games. An unlicensed Atari 2600 game in 1983. Michael Myers appears as a playable killer in Dead by Daylight (2016), whose "Evil Within" stalking mechanic — power accrues by watching, not by chasing — is the best mechanical translation of the character anyone has produced.

Documentary. Halloween: 25 Years of Terror and the Halloween Unmasked podcast are the standard behind-the-scenes sources.

10CRAFT NOTES

The load-bearing question

Every Halloween film is a wager on one question: should the evil be explained? The 1978 film says no and is the best of them. Curse says yes and buries a timeline. Ends says "it's contagious" and splits the audience down the middle. Whatever you write, decide your answer on page one and never wobble, because the wobble is what killed most of these films — not the answer itself.

Structural options, with their costs

  • Motiveless. Maximum dread, minimum character. You'll need someone else to carry interiority.
  • Familial. Instant stakes, instant shrinkage — the horror becomes a family matter and stops being about the night.
  • Cursed / ritual. Rich worldbuilding, but every ounce of explanation costs a degree of fear. Michael becomes a tool, and tools aren't frightening.
  • Etiological. Sympathy, texture, and the risk that you've written a sad case study wearing a mask.
  • Contagious. Modern, thematically alive, and structurally treacherous — the moment anyone can be the killer, the specific one stops mattering.

What's exhausted

The mob. The hospital corridor. The legacy survivor in a fortified house. The podcast framing device. The "he was here the whole time" reveal. All fine, all done to death.

What's barely touched

The institution as antagonist. The years between kills. Anyone who isn't a Strode. Halloween outside Illinois. Non-white, non-suburban, non-teenage victims. The economics of a town whose only cultural export is a murder house. What it costs to be the person who didn't die — twenty years later, in a support group, in an insurance claim, in a deposition.

A frame you already have

If you want a lens with teeth: the 1978 film is a nearly perfect mortality-salience event. Loomis is a man who spent fifteen years building a symbolic defense — diagnosis, terminology, a professional worldview — against a thing that refuses to be symbolized, and when it escapes, his language collapses into theology. Laurie survives because she stops trying to make meaning and simply acts. The town's response, in Kills, is textbook worldview defense: identify an out-group, chant a slogan, kill the wrong man. The franchise has been circling that machinery for forty-eight years without ever naming it. You could name it.

Practical constraints if you write in-universe

  • Michael doesn't talk. If your story needs an interior voice, it belongs to someone else.
  • He doesn't kill children. Violating this reads as a different character.
  • He goes home. If your story is set elsewhere, you need a reason strong enough to overcome the house's gravity.
  • The mask has to be found, not bought. Its acquisition is always a scene.
  • The date is fixed. October 31, or an October 30 that spills over. This is a constraint and also a gift: you get a hard clock for free.
